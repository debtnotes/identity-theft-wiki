Decision tree: should you buy identity-theft insurance?

This flow is a buying framework, not a coverage determination. Stop and obtain the actual certificate/policy whenever the answer turns on an exclusion, deadline, state, or a known incident.

flowchart TD A[Start: review accounts and credit reports] --> B{Known suspicious activity?} B -->|Yes| C[Report to affected institution and IdentityTheft.gov] C --> D[Ask provider in writing about pre-enrollment restoration and insurance] D --> E{Written answer covers the fact pattern?} E -->|No / unclear| F[Do not buy for that existing event; use free recovery tools and seek qualified advice if needed] E -->|Yes| G[Compare exact certificate, deadlines, and limits] B -->|No| H[Freeze credit files; enable account alerts and MFA] H --> I{Existing employer, bank, card, or home-policy benefit?} I -->|Yes| J[Read the benefit and other-insurance clause] I -->|No| K[Compare paid plans only for desired restoration and verified eligible losses] J --> K G --> L{Value comes from verified terms or restoration help?} K --> L L -->|No| M[Use free controls and revisit later] L -->|Yes| N[Buy only after saving policy, price, renewal, and cancellation terms]

The decision hinges on four questions

  1. Is this protection for a future event or an existing problem? A new subscription may not cover an event already discovered.
  2. What cannot be recovered elsewhere? Banks, card issuers, employers, and home-policy endorsements may be primary or duplicate sources of help.
  3. What service is being purchased? Monitoring, hands-on restoration, and insurance reimbursement are different products.
  4. Can the insurer prove the loss is covered? Read notice, proof, consent, other-insurance, and deadline provisions before paying.

Evidence note: FTC and CFPB describe free freezes, reports, fraud alerts, and IdentityTheft.gov recovery tools. Those controls are the starting point, not an add-on after buying a subscription.
